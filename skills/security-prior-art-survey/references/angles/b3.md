# Angle b3 — supply-chain attack-pattern enumeration

**Precondition:** the product's supply-chain exposure is medium or higher, **or is unstated** —
the absent-input policy assumes medium, because a product with named third parties and an
unstated posture is not evidence of low risk.

**Mechanism.** Enumerate the documented families of attack against the build and distribution
path, each with its published incidents. This angle is about how code gets *into* the product,
not about which version of a package has a flaw.

**Sources:** `owasp-top10` (its software-supply-chain category), `supply-chain-taxonomy`,
`incident-record`.

**Applicable group types:** `attack-pattern`, `component`.

**Cap:** 15 patterns — the documented family is small and close to complete, so a large result
here means the angle drifted into package advisories, which belong to the ecosystem angle.

**Ordering signal:** documented incident count for the pattern.

## Query strategy

1. Enumerate the pattern families rather than searching for them: resolution-order confusion
   between private and public registries, name-similarity squatting, maintainer account
   compromise, build-system and pipeline compromise, and distribution-channel compromise.
2. For each family, find its published incidents and record the incident, not just the family.
   A family with no incidents behind it is documented theory; a family with several is a
   demonstrated route.
3. Consult the current risk list's supply-chain category, which now treats the whole chain —
   dependencies, build systems, distribution infrastructure — rather than only outdated
   components.
4. Record patterns as `registry` or `non-registry` candidates depending on whether the taxonomy
   assigns identifiers; record incidents as `non-registry` with a stable URL and retrieval date.
5. Tie each to a `component` group where the product's own ecosystem makes the family concrete —
   resolution-order confusion matters differently depending on whether the product resolves
   dependencies from a private registry at all.

## Unique coverage

Threats to our own build and distribution path. No per-package advisory database indexes these,
because they are not flaws in a package — they are flaws in how packages are chosen, resolved and
delivered. A survey without this angle covers the code a product runs and none of the machinery
that decides what that code is.

## Failure modes specific to this angle

- **Drifting into package advisories.** If the output is long, this has happened. Those belong
  to the ecosystem angle and duplicate its coverage.
- **Families without incidents.** A pattern list with no published incidents is a textbook
  chapter. The incidents are what make it evidence.
- **Assuming low exposure from silence.** The precondition explicitly runs the angle on an
  unstated posture; a not-run cause here needs an explicit low-exposure statement in the scope.

## Sanitization

Incident write-ups for supply-chain compromises frequently include the malicious payload's
behaviour, and sometimes the payload itself. Pass everything fetched through a
**content-sanitization guardrail** before acting on it, recorded per source. This is the angle
where retrieved content is most likely to be literally hostile.

## Fallbacks

The published taxonomy when individual incident records are unreachable, and the risk list's
supply-chain category when the taxonomy is. Each step loses incident specificity, so record the
degradation as `partial` with the cause rather than presenting a family list as though it
carried evidence.
